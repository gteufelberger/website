Satellites are cool.
Thanks to measurements done by satellites orbiting our planet, we can estimate plant health from space!

To do this, we can make use of a value called _normialised difference vegetation index_, or NDVI for short.

For this we only need two channels, red and near-infrared.
The reason we use those two is that when plants are active, i.e. they perform photosynthesis, they absorb most of the red light.
At the same time plants strongly reflect near-infrared light due to their cell structure.

This means that a low energy signal on the red band and a high energy signal on the near-infrared implies a healthy plant.
Neat!

To actually calculate NDVI we use the following formula:

$$
\text{NDVI} = \frac{\text{near-infrared} - \text{red}}{\text{near-infrared} + \text{red}}
$$

This gives us a value between $-1$ and $+1$, where $+1$ implies a very healthy (active) plant while values closer to $-1$ mean a dead plant or non-plant object like rocks and water.

Comparing these values with the visible light bands can show as whether what looks to be a green and healthy forest is actually healthy or not.
Cool!

To see it in action you can use for example the [Copernicus browser](https://browser.dataspace.copernicus.eu/) which gives you access to public satellite data and allows you to look at specific preprocessed layers, such as NDVI.
